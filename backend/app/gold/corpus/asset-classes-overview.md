title: "Where money can go: the household asset-class map"

Every rupee a household sets aside sits in one of a handful of asset classes, and each
class trades off three things: safety of the capital, the return it can earn, and how
quickly you can get the money back (liquidity). No class wins on all three, which is
why a plan spreads money across several rather than chasing one.

The safety end holds cash, savings accounts, and liquid funds — money you can reach in
a day, earning little, meant for spending and emergencies, not growth. Next are fixed
deposits and small-savings schemes (PPF, EPF, post-office): capital is very safe and
the return is fixed, but the money is locked for a term and the return often barely
beats inflation after tax.

The growth end holds equity — ownership in companies, bought directly as shares or,
more cheaply and simply, through index funds that track a whole market like the Nifty
or Sensex. Equity carries real short-term risk (it can fall 30–50% in a bad year) but
has historically delivered the highest long-run return, which only matters over
horizons of seven years or more.

In between sit bonds (lending money for a fixed coupon), gold (a hedge that holds value
when currencies and markets wobble), and real estate (large, illiquid, part-consumption).
Commodities beyond gold rarely belong in a household plan.

Decision relevance: the right mix is set by how soon you need the money and how much
short-term fall you can tolerate — not by which class is "best." NextRupee ranks across
classes and never names a specific fund, stock, or product.
