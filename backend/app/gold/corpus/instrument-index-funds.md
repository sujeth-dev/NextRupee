title: "Index funds and ETFs: owning the whole market at low cost"
tags: [instrument, index, growth]
---

An index fund holds every company in a market index — the Nifty 50 or the Sensex, for
example — in the same proportions, so its return simply mirrors that market rather than
trying to beat it. An ETF (exchange-traded fund) does the same but trades like a share
on the exchange. Both give a household broad equity ownership in a single, cheap holding.

Their advantage is cost and discipline. Because no manager is picking stocks, the annual
fee (expense ratio) is very low — often around 0.1–0.3%, against roughly 1–2% for an
actively managed fund. That gap compounds: over decades, the cost drag of high fees can
quietly consume a large share of the final corpus, which is why low-cost index funds
tend to outperform most active funds over long periods. They also remove selection
risk — you are guaranteed the market's return, never the manager's mistake.

The trade-off is that you also get the market's full falls; an index fund drops just as
hard as the market in a crash and offers no downside protection. Regular fixed
investments into an index fund (a SIP) turn that volatility into an advantage, buying
more units when prices are low.

Decision relevance: for most households seeking equity exposure, a low-cost broad-market
index fund is the simplest, cheapest route, which is why it anchors the growth slice of
many plans. NextRupee refers to the class and its cost logic, and never recommends a
particular index fund or fund house.
